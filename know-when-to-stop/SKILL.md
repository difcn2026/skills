# Know When to Stop

When a project, direction, or relationship is at a continue-or-stop threshold, two errors compete: counting everything already invested as a reason to continue (sunk-cost fallacy), and counting none of it (marginal analysis). Both share one hidden assumption — that what you've invested is homogeneous. This skill breaks that assumption: it distills the investment into a sunk part and a transferable part. The sunk part never enters the decision; the transferable part enters not as a reason to continue, but as **the benefit of stopping**. Stopping stops being "admitting failure" and becomes "ending one direction + keeping the assets".

## When this triggers

- Decision language contains lock-in markers: "we've come too far", "can't stop now", "can't let it go to waste", "stopping means admitting failure".
- Marginal analysis has already concluded "stop", but emotional resistance to the investment keeps the decision suspended.
- The investment contains heterogeneous parts (money, time, relationships, skills, identity).
- You are at a continue/stop threshold: project termination, direction change, relationship end, career transition.

## Steps

1. **Catch the lock-in signal** — identify the sunk-cost lock-in markers, and record the exact emotional phrasing. This is not a bias to erase; it is an identity signpost pointing to which investments have entered the user's identity layer vs which are just tools.

2. **Distill the investment** — list every invested component and classify each as sunk or transferable. **Transferable classification (three questions, all must pass)**:
   | Question | Pass condition |
   |----------|---------------|
   | ① Can it move? — can this investment detach from the current direction and be used directly in a new one? | yes → pass; no → sunk |
   | ② Is there a receiver? — does a *specific* new direction exist that would use it? | yes → pass; "might be useful someday" → sunk |
   | ③ Is there a use case? — can you say "how it gets used in scenario X"? | yes → pass; no → sunk |

   **Minimal example**:
   ```
   Project: a two-year app, deciding whether to stop.
   Invested components, distilled one by one:

   - $2M development spend        → ① can't move (money is spent) → sunk
   - 80k lines of code            → ① can move ② has a receiver (login module reusable) ③ can name the use → transferable
   - accumulated growth methods   → ①②③ all pass → transferable
   - the team's industry know-how → transferable
   - the founder's "this app must succeed" identity → identity, travels with the person → transferable (identity signpost)
   - time already spent (unrecoverable) → sunk
   ```

3. **Compute the transfer ratio** — `transferable / total invested`. A higher ratio means stopping loses less, and stopping is less a failure.

4. **Marginal stop criterion** — look only at the future margin. **Three-step net value method**:
   1. List the next 12 months of *continuing* marginal cash flow (revenue − cost), discount at 10%, sum = continue value.
   2. List the next 12 months of *stopped* cash flow (the new direction's marginal return + what the transferable assets produce there), same discount, sum = stop value.
   3. Compare: continue > stop → continue; stop > continue → stop and reorganize.
   **The sunk components enter neither side** — this is what separates this skill from plain financial analysis.

5. **Produce a recommendation**:
   - **Continue**: marginal net gain > 0 and continuing increases the density of transferable assets.
   - **Stop and reorganize**: marginal net gain < 0; give the transferable-asset list and the reorganization path.
   - **Neither (fake-continue alarm)**: continuing only adds sunk parts without raising transferable density.

6. **Output the distillation report** — sunk list (release), transferable list (carry), stop criterion, reorganization path. End every report with: *"You are not stopping a project; you are stopping one direction of what you invested. What you carry away is the part of yourself that direction already turned into."*

## Boundaries

- Do not decide for the user — provide the distillation and the criterion; the decision is the user's.
- Do not force emotion away — emotional entanglement is recorded as an identity signpost, but never used as a lock-in reason.
- Transferability is judged on current known information, not on black-swan predictions.
- Do not process external market pricing — only classify the investment and frame the future margin.
- Do not auto-execute the reorganization — the path is a suggestion, not an order.

## Counter (recovery table)

| Failure | Detect | Recover |
|---------|--------|---------|
| Distillation bias (sunk misjudged as transferable) | After stopping, the asset can't actually move | Re-check the three questions, mark it "pseudo-transferable", fix the rule |
| Marginal calc ossified (slipped into finance-only) | Output is only numbers, no emotional-entanglement capture | Re-inject the identity-signpost capture |
| Lock-in not released | User says "but I still can't let go" | Trigger deeper identity-asset identification: find which identity investment "can't let go" maps to, convert it to a carryable asset |
| Everything marked transferable (pain avoidance) | Transfer ratio unrealistically high | Force cross-check: each transferable component must name a specific use case in the new direction |
| Reorganization path empty | No new direction exists after stopping | Downgrade to marginal analysis: give the stop criterion, mark the path "to explore", do not fabricate one |
